Before I begin, Autism and ADHD is different for everyone. I make some general statements here, but remember they don’t apply to everyone. This was written in one session, without planning where it would go. The style of writing changes as my emotions grew stronger throughout writing; I could edit it, but that is just as important to convey.

As someone who was diagnosed with Autism and ADHD (abbreviated to AuDHD) in my mid 40s, I'm still learning to understand what being AuDHD means on a practical day-to-day level. Over the last six months, or so, I've been starting to understand my energy levels, and my resilience.
<!--more-->

I know the word "energy" can sound wooish, but I use it in a similar way to spoons in [spoon theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoon_theory), but it could also be thought of as my level of resilience. It's just a shortcut that I use to explain to others. Other phrases I use to describe low resilience is being deregulated, or in a heightened state.

Another way that people describe energy is imagining a bucket with some water in it. If you add a rock in the bucket, the water level rises, and eventually overflows. If you imagine the rocks being little things that reduce my resilience. This does a better job of describing it than spoons, but it still doesn’t quite feel right to me. For example, if I am low on resilience, something that would normally only have a small affect will have a disproportionately larger effect on further eroding my resilience. A better analogy for me is, instead of picturing using a bucket, picture a [conical scientific flask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlenmeyer_flask). This better demonstrates that if the water level is low, a rock will raise it only a little, but the higher the water level, the more of an effect a rock of the same size has.

It would be nice if recovery worked by removing stones from the flask and the first drops will be larger, but unfortunately, that’s not how it works. Recovery is a slow process that also works like a flask, where when initial gains are slow, and over time more and more gains have a larger effect.

## What do I need energy for anyway?

One of the big things I need to have energy for is [masking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autistic_masking). To me, masking is a whole range of behaviours required to function in a world where the average neurotypical person will incorrectly assume that they are talking another neurotypical person, and will expect the other person to act in line with their expectations of normal behaviour. Unfortunately, when someone communicates with someone whose brain works differently to theirs, while expecting it to work in a similar way, at best, there will be miscommunications, at worst, there will be negative impressions (stupid, deliberately obstructionist, annoyingly pedantic, just to list a few).

Autistics are generally considered to have issues in social communication, but research has shown that this is only an [issue when communicating with non-autistics](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361320919286). Of course the majority of the people in society are non-autistic, and by default will expect that they are talking to another non-autistic.

A lot of “helpful” advice to autistics in the area of social communication is to do simple things that everyone else does. Unfortunately, if your brain doesn’t work the same way, these things aren’t simple. It takes a lot of energy to remember and actively concentrate on things like

- to keep eye contact
- make sure that when you are keeping eye contact, to not come across as creepy
- monitor what the other person isn’t saying - omissions that are expected to be understood, tone of voice, phrases that have been learnt to mean something that the words don’t convey, expressions, gestures, tone of voice, etc.
- monitor your own expressions, tone of voice, body language
- remember that what you consider helpful responses will be taken by others as rude or direct
- don’t [stim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimming) or fidget, or do something else to help me pay attention so I don’t appear rude
- remember that people like fluff surrounding their communications, and prefer not to start a conversation directly with what you wanted to talk about
- monitor the conversation for points that I can contribute my thoughts
- monitor my posture
- manage emotions and frustrations.

These things are things that tend to come naturally to non-autistics, and they don’t understand that we need to spend extra brainpower fighting our natural instinct to communicate naturally and authentically, and monitor everything both we and the other person are doing. When energy is low, masking becomes harder, which then result in miscommunications and reactions that we don’t understand, which then becomes frustrating, which then uses more energy.

A common assumption is that ADHD is a lack of focus. This can’t be further from the truth. The struggle with ADHD is to _control_ where our focus goes. If fighting our natural instincts to force our focus onto an activity that isn’t where our focus wants to be at that time, also takes large amounts of energy. The helpful advice is “just do something else”, without realising that this is the same problem. I’ve once heard ADHD focus described as a steam train. It takes a while to get going, but once it’s going, it will be our source of focus. Like any steam train, it is possible to derail ADHD’s focus once it’s going. Meetings, Teams messages, calls all are great ways of making sure that the steam train will need to start from zero again. There is nothing as frustrating as a 30 minute break between meetings.

Finally, day to day living requires energy. I can be physically exhausted at the end of the day, even if I’ve had enough sleep over recent nights, and will need to nap. This isn’t about enough sleep, it’s the brain saying that it needs to shut off for a while. It doesn’t help with general tiredness, but is just enough to be able to function for the rest of the evening.

## Okay, so what actually reduces my energy?

Autistics don’t cope well with sudden changes. We have pictured, and usually rehearsed what our day will look like, or what we will need to do to accomplish a task, or even what finished will even look like for a task. When this is changed, it takes a toll on us. Unexpectedly saying that we are going out when we’ve pictured a day at home is stressful. Likewise, other people helping us accomplish a task, and doing it in a different way to what we expected, or skipping steps that we consider important, because they don’t value them can be stressful.

“Helpful” comments that suggest that we need to be adaptable, or that’s just how things work, are worse than helpful. They tell us that because non-autistics don’t need this structure in their life, that it can’t be important for people who need this structure, and therefore we need to adapt. So we need to spend more energy for dealign with a society that doesn’t respect our needs.

Related to this is all our need for routine. I have a morning routine before work. Others will look at it, and not see why I can’t change the order that I do things. If a tradie is coming at 8:30 I am expected to re-arrange my routine, without any consideration given to how important this routine and structure is to help me through my day. But, because non-autistics can just shift their routine without it being a big deal, it’s just expected that the same applies for autistics, not matter how many times we explain that there is a cost to this change.

Organisation is important for Autistics. People will look at my environment and see chaos, and to a degree they made be right - ADHD can cause issues with remembering to tidy up, but there is a big difference between tidiness and organisation. Someone can look at something lying on a desk that needs to be put away. This is untidy, so they open the closet drawer, and put it in there. It’s now magically tidy. But it’s been placed in a random spot, rather than organised. To me, this is worse than just being out in the open, because it’s now in a drawer that has a different purpose, and I won’t be able to find it; it’s not where I expect to find it. There is a big difference between hiding stuff so it’s not untidy, and putting things where they belong. I need to be in an environment where things that are not where they belong are out in the open as a reminder that they need to be put where they belong. This may seem like a small thing to non-autistics, but it is an important part of managing our environment, and dealing with these things saps energy.

The things I mentioned in the previous section also take their toll on how, much energy we have. And this section is far from a complete list. Noisy environments, unexpected conversations, having to wait for something (queue, being early, etc.), people doing things incorrectly (read the damn manual), people violating our strong sense of justice, the expectation that because something takes no effort for someone that it also takes no effort for us, needing to validate our own existence, etc. are just a few more things.

## So what changes now?

Honestly nothing. Society is so far away from understanding neurodiversity, and understand how our needs are different. Even if everyone I know reads this and changes their behaviour or expectations of me, it won’t make a difference to the next person who is going through the same thing. And you may not be able to identify the next person you interact with who is Autistic, and who is going through something similar. But until society as a whole gains an understanding that other’s minds work differently, the implications of that, and not having the expectation that all people they interact with are just like them and acting accordingly, it won’t make a difference.
